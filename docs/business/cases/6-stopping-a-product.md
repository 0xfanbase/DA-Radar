# Case 6: Stopping a product

*Fictional case. All people and firms are invented. Facts about Hong Kong rules are real and cited.*

## The situation

Kittiwake Bank is a fictional Hong Kong bank. It is a registered institution (RI), a bank registered with the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) for securities business. It holds virtual assets (VA) such as ether for private-banking clients.

Two years ago Kittiwake launched a staking service. Staking means locking client tokens so a validator can help run a blockchain and earn rewards [S:4641e6c404d2, p.1]. A validator is a firm that runs the computers confirming new blocks (concept). Kittiwake also lends to margin clients so they can buy virtual assets.

The staking service has lost money for four quarters. Rewards have fallen, and the bank's costs have not. The chief executive asks you to lead a review: stop, restructure or keep the service.

## Cast

- **You**: head of compliance, digital assets.
- **Oona Pebblemoor**: chief executive (CEO). She wants a decision within one quarter.
- **Felix Ferrywhistle**: chief financial officer (CFO). He owns the profit and loss account (P&L), the record of income and costs.
- **Ruth Harbourlane**: chief risk officer (CRO). She owns the bank's risk appetite.
- **Sanjay Gullsby**: head of private banking. Many of his clients use the service.
- **Pebblecrest Validators**: a fictional outside firm that runs validators for Kittiwake.
- **The supervisor**: the bank's Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) case team. It acts only as the HKMA's published expectations describe.

## Exhibit A: the rules that apply

- A bank offering staking should keep control of everything needed to take client assets out of staking [S:4641e6c404d2, section (A) and fn 2, p.2]. Examples are withdrawal keys and exit messages signed in advance [S:4641e6c404d2, section (A) and fn 2, p.2].
- The bank should disclose fees, lock-up periods, and the unstaking process and how long it takes [S:4641e6c404d2, section (B), p.2]. It should also disclose the risks and how related losses would be handled [S:4641e6c404d2, section (B), p.2].
- If the bank outsources staking, it should check the provider first and keep monitoring it [S:4641e6c404d2, section (C), p.3].
- A firm that plans to stop or suspend staking should put clients' best interests at the centre of the decision [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.6]. It should tell all clients the material facts about the exit, in good time [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.6]. That includes the decision, the options open to clients, such as unstaking, and any material changes during the exit [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.6, Note].
- The firm should have operating procedures for events that harm staked assets, including starting withdrawals from staking [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.4].
- Intermediaries should notify the SFC, and the HKMA where it applies, before changing their VA activities [S:b79266f3da51, para 10]. The list includes other material changes to the arrangements first notified [S:b79266f3da51, para 10(e)].

## Act 1: Stop, restructure or keep?

Felix shows the numbers in Exhibit B. Sanjay argues that staking keeps custody clients at the bank. Pebblecrest offers a lower fee if Kittiwake signs a three-year contract.

### Decision 1: What happens to the staking service?

- **A.** Stop: close the service to all clients and unstake every position.
- **B.** Restructure: keep the service, sign Pebblecrest's cheaper contract, raise the bank's share of rewards and support fewer tokens.
- **C.** Narrow: keep the service for institutional clients only and close it to everyone else.

### Discussion notes

- **Trade-offs.** Option A ends the losses but may cost custody balances and client goodwill. Option B keeps clients and lowers costs, but ties the bank to one vendor for three years. Option C keeps the largest accounts and ends the risk acknowledgements needed from other clients.
- **What the rules require.** Any exit decision is expected to take due account of clients' best interests [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.6]. Under Option B, the bank keeps checking and monitoring Pebblecrest as an outsourced provider [S:4641e6c404d2, section (C), p.3]. A higher bank share changes what clients receive. Fees, and how returns are worked out, are part of the information the bank should give clients [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.7(c)-(d)]. Under Option C, institutional and qualified corporate professional investors do not need to sign a risk acknowledgement, but other clients do [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.8].
- **What a strong answer covers.** It sets out the loss for each option over two or three years, not one quarter. It counts custody fees at risk if clients leave. It weighs vendor lock-in against lower cost. It asks whether narrowing the client base is itself a change the regulators need to hear about first [S:b79266f3da51, para 10(a)].
- **Traps.** Judging staking as a stand-alone profit line when it partly supports custody. Treating Option C as low effort: the bank still has to exit the smaller clients fairly [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.6].

## Act 2: Treating clients on the way out

The committee picks an option that closes staking to at least some clients. Ruth reminds everyone that unstaking takes time on some networks. During that wait, the assets earn nothing and cannot move.

### Decision 2: How does Kittiwake take clients out of staking?

- **A.** Fixed date: unstake every affected position on one date, after written notice.
- **B.** Client choice: give clients a window to withdraw, move assets elsewhere or accept unstaking by the bank. Unstake the rest at the end.
- **C.** Transfer: offer to move staked positions to another staking provider, such as an SFC-licensed platform, for clients who agree.

### Discussion notes

- **Trade-offs.** Option A is simple and quick to run, but gives clients little control over timing. Option B respects client choice but runs for longer and costs more to operate. Option C keeps clients earning rewards, but adds a new firm and a transfer process.
- **What the rules require.** The firm should tell clients the exit decision, their options and any material changes along the way [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.6, Note]. Firms should stake client assets only through an SFC-licensed platform or a bank [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.2]. Before using a provider, the firm should make sure it has SFC approval or has discussed its plans with the HKMA [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.3]. Staking should follow the client's standing authority or a one-off written direction [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.5].
- **What a strong answer covers.** It lists each network's unstaking period and tells clients plainly. It checks that the bank still controls the withdrawal keys and pre-signed exit messages [S:4641e6c404d2, section (A) and fn 2, p.2]. It plans for a slashing event (a network penalty that takes part of the staked tokens) during the exit (concept). It says who bears such a loss, as the disclosure standard expects [S:4641e6c404d2, section (B), p.2].
- **Traps.** Assuming clients can take their assets back the next day. Moving positions under Option C without fresh client direction. Forgetting the reward payout arrangements the bank disclosed, which still apply to rewards earned before unstaking [S:4641e6c404d2, section (B), p.2].

## Act 3: Telling the regulators, and the sister product

Oona wants one announcement. Felix raises a second product: VA margin financing, which also lost money this year. Some staking clients use it. He asks whether to act on both at once.

### Decision 3: How does Kittiwake handle notice and the financing book?

- **A.** Notify the SFC and the HKMA about the staking change before client letters go out. Review margin financing separately next year.
- **B.** Send the regulators one pack before any client letters. It covers the staking exit and a plan to accept only securities as collateral for new VA financing.
- **C.** Announce to clients first, finish the exit, then report to the regulators once it is complete.

### Discussion notes

- **Trade-offs.** Option A keeps the staking exit simple and leaves the financing losses running. Option B deals with both at once but makes one larger change to explain. Option C is fastest for clients but puts the regulators after the event.
- **What the rules require.** The SFC and the HKMA remind intermediaries to notify them before changing VA activities [S:b79266f3da51, para 10]. For VA financing, a bank should consider carefully whether relying on VA collateral is prudent [S:e64b7b0267b2, item (4), p.3]. Loans backed only by virtual assets must be treated as clean (unsecured) loans for capital [S:e64b7b0267b2, item (9), p.5]. The bank should not repledge or reuse VA collateral except to enforce it on default [S:e64b7b0267b2, item (7)(c), p.5].
- **What a strong answer covers.** It sets out the order of events in each option and tests it against the notice expected before a change [S:b79266f3da51, para 10]. It asks whether a change to financing collateral counts as a material change to the arrangements first notified [S:b79266f3da51, para 10(e)]. It shows how margin calls and limits work while collateral terms change [S:e64b7b0267b2, item (8), p.5]. It gives existing borrowers time and clear notice.
- **Traps.** Treating notice as paperwork done after the decision. Letting the financing review delay the staking exit, or the reverse. Forgetting that financing is open only to the bank's securities margin financing clients [S:e64b7b0267b2, item (2), p.2].

## What this case teaches

- Stopping a product is a regulated event, not only a business choice. Clients' interests and timely disclosure shape the exit [S:31b4f67e0233, para 8.6].
- Exits cost money. In Exhibit B, the exit year looks worse than carrying on.
- The unstaking wait is a client-treatment issue, not only an operations issue.
- Notice to the regulators comes before a change, not after it [S:b79266f3da51, para 10].
- A review of one product often exposes another. Each has its own rules.
